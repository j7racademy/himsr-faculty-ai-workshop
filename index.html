FIRST, A SHARED LANGUAGE

What is AI?
What are we using today?

Start with the family of technologies, then distinguish tools that identify patterns from tools that generate new material. We will use generative tools for learning, teaching, and research—with faculty checking every consequential claim.

Artificial intelligenceThe broad field: systems that perform tasks associated with human intelligence.
Machine learningA part of AI that learns patterns from data instead of following only hand-written rules.
Deep learningA part of machine learning built with layered neural networks.
Large language modelsDeep learning models trained on large amounts of language data; they can help power generative chat tools.
PATTERN FINDING

Radiology or pathology AI

A model may detect, classify, or mark a finding in an image. Its output must be evaluated for the intended clinical task and population.

CONTENT GENERATION

Generative chat tools

A model may draft a case, explain a concept, summarize sources, or propose a research outline. A fluent answer is still a draft to check.

These examples describe different tasks, not separate worlds: modern multimodal models can work with images and text.

THE PROMPTING HABIT · 12 MIN

Give the tool enough to work with.

State the task, audience, source boundary, output format, and review check. You can revise the prompt after seeing the first result.

Instead of “Make a lecture on diabetes”

“For Phase II MBBS students, use only this approved teaching handout to draft a five-minute explanation of diabetic nephropathy. Give three learning points, one case question, and a source note. Mark anything the handout does not support.”

5 MIN FRAME15 MIN DEMO + TRY5 MIN DISCUSS
THE PROMPT / COPY & USE

Prepare an exploratory literature scoping brief (2022–2026) on cost-effective biomarkers and AI-assisted risk stratification for early diabetic nephropathy in South Asian populations. Prioritize peer-reviewed sources, PubMed/PMC, and relevant Indian guidance. Summarize study designs, populations and sample sizes, reported diagnostic metrics only when explicit, limitations, and three research gaps. Provide working links for each factual claim, distinguish evidence from inference, and flag missing or conflicting evidence. Do not call this a systematic review.

Use a completed report so the room never waits for generation. A scoping brief is a starting point; a formal review needs a documented search strategy, eligibility criteria, and independent appraisal.

THE HUMAN CHECK

Useful AI. Clear
responsibility.

Before an output reaches a classroom, chart, protocol, or paper, the responsible faculty member checks it and follows institutional policy.

01 / PRIVACY

Protect patient information

Use fictional, public, or institutionally approved material. Do not enter identifiable HAHC patient details into public AI tools.

02 / EVIDENCE

Verify sources and claims

Open citations, check clinical details, and flag what the source cannot support.

03 / CONTEXT

Check local relevance

Review Indian guidance, populations, and the institution’s own procedures before use.

04 / ACCOUNTABILITY

Own the final work

Disclose AI assistance where required and keep the clinician, educator, or investigator responsible for the final decision.

DISCUSS · 5 MIN

A resident has a ward summary and wants a teaching case.

What should happen before any text is pasted into an AI tool?

Discuss: use a fictional case or an institutionally approved and appropriately de-identified one; confirm permissions and tool policy; review every generated fact before teaching.
GOVERNANCE IN PRACTICE

Three decisions to make before using the output

Teaching: Who reviews an AI-generated question before students see it?

Clinical material: Which data and which approved tools may be used?

Research: Who checks citations, methods, and any required disclosure?
